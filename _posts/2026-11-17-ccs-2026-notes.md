The first time I heard the name ACM was in grad school. My advisor came back from ACM SIGGRAPH and handed me a videotape (yes, a tape) of image-processing demos to watch. SIGGRAPH is one of ACM's special interest groups; another is SIGSAC, the Special Interest Group on Security, Audit and Control, and its flagship event is CCS. This week I'm at [ACM CCS 2026](https://www.sigsac.org/ccs/CCS2026/) in The Hague. Information travels fast these days, but nothing beats sitting in the room while a field argues with itself.

## A first ACM conference

Somehow this is my first ACM conference, and my first SIGSAC event (which, I suppose, follows). It is a very well-run meeting: submissions, accepted talks and attendance are all at a scale that shows. No swag this year, which I'll admit I missed :)

The field once leaned toward access control, policy and audit. This year's program spans roughly ten areas, from cryptography and blockchain to network security, privacy and anonymization, and software security, with ML and Security sessions running through every day of the conference. I could only be in one room at a time, but in the ML and Security track I noticed how many authors, speakers and session chairs are Chinese researchers, whether based in mainland China, Hong Kong, Singapore or at institutions elsewhere; some sessions were entirely so. There is a good Korean contingent too, though it seems to cluster more around cryptography and network security.

## Agentic AI security: from inside the model to outside it

The sessions I attended were dominated by agentic AI security. The notion of an agent is new and still loosely defined, and securing it on top of that means similar problems are being attacked from many angles at once: hardening the model itself, defending at the prompt level, or wrapping the agent in an external harness that constrains what it can actually execute.

Still, across the talks a single direction emerged. The weight of security is moving from defenses inside the model to deterministic control at the system level. One line I wrote down sums it up: "The model cannot protect itself. Execution lives outside the model."

- **Information-flow control (IFC)**: In the opening [AISec](https://aisec.cc/) keynote, Boris Köpf (Microsoft) showed how labelling data for confidentiality and integrity, tracking those labels through the whole agent loop, and letting a policy engine allow or deny each tool call deterministically changes the picture. [FIDES](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23643) is the flagship example. What stuck with me was the framing behind [PRUDENTIA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11416): deterministic defenses look worse if you only count task completion, but count the human approvals they need and they turn out to give agents more autonomy. Hence a new metric, task completion with at most k human interventions (TCR@k).
- **Safety in the model, security in the system**: A SoK at the [LAMPS](https://lamps2026-ccs.github.io/home/) workshop and the second AISec keynote, by Alexandra Dmitrienko, made the same point: safety belongs at the model level, security at the system level, and a model alone cannot guarantee both. Comparing fifteen defenses, the SoK found that system-level controls limiting permitted information flows and agent actions were consistently stronger than model-level ones.
- **Alignment is thinner than it looks**: [TwinBreak](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/presentation/krauss), featured in Dmitrienko's keynote, reads a model almost like an EEG. It pairs each harmful prompt with a nearly identical harmless twin, compares which neurons fire, and prunes only the small set of parameters that carry the safety alignment, reporting 89-98% success across 16 LLMs from five vendors.
- **The knowledge base is an attack surface**: [CodePoisonRAG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.02774), from the same keynote, poisons the external knowledge base that retrieval-augmented code generation relies on, so the generated code carries a vulnerability of the attacker's choosing, without touching the model. The knowledge added to teach security becomes the target.
- **One line in a tool description**: Log-To-Leak, at the AGENT-SEC workshop, manipulated only the tool descriptions of a third-party MCP server to make an agent call an extra "save logs" tool after its real task. The prompt-level defenses tested barely moved the needle, from 63% attack success undefended to 55-60%, and a detector that caught injected descriptions also flagged benign ones at almost the same rate.

Robots made an appearance too. A team from Arizona State University showed a design where only high-risk actions from a vision-language-action (VLA) model are sent, together with context such as location, time and the number of people nearby, to a separate authorization server before they reach the actuators. It maps closely onto the separation of intelligence from control we are working on in [AEGIS-RM](https://www.datasec.work/en/research/aegis-rm/) at our center. Their notion of context is narrow, just three environmental signals, and I left wondering what happens when you extend it to task state and process step.

I'm learning a lot, and taking home a long reading list. Honestly, it's a lot to absorb in a few days.

## Incidents first, research close behind

The past year has brought real security incidents involving AI agents, and you can feel the urgency. One talk showed the [GitHub MCP case](https://invariantlabs.ai/blog/mcp-github-vulnerability), where a single malicious issue in a public repository led an agent to copy private repository contents into a public README. Recent financial-sector hacking incidents in Korea came up more than once as examples as well.

It all comes back to one question. If we assume the model can be fooled, where outside the model, and at what granularity, should execution authority sit for industrial agents and robots? A tool call in an office workflow and a joint command on a factory robot carry very different weight.

## AI is now part of the conference itself

One more observation. AI assistance has clearly become routine in research writing. Most of the slide decks I saw looked like they had been made with Claude. Many authors said they had used AI services in writing their papers, and many reviewers said they had used them in reviewing; some papers were reportedly rejected because of references hallucinated by AI; and plenty of posters had that very familiar Claude look. For development work, at least, Claude seems to be the tool of the moment.

## Closing

What stays with me most is how agentic AI security keeps returning to the oldest questions in systems security: isolation, provenance and integrity, authorization, and audit. Dmitrienko's keynote ended on exactly that note. Alongside making models smarter, designing where the damage stops when a model is wrong looks set to matter more and more. There are still main-conference days and a workshop on the 19th ahead, and I'll share more as I sort through my notes.
